    The bigger fix is the power itself

    Almost all of AI video's footprint is electricity, and so is VFX rendering's, so a clean grid shrinks both directly. Wētā's Avatar rendering would have emitted about a fifth as much on New Zealand's grid as it did on Australia's. Clean power won't remove the water used for cooling or the carbon in the hardware, but it would remove most of the rest.

    Filmmaking can't get there on clean electricity alone. Crews and gear travel by plane and truck, and there's no electric replacement yet for most of the planes in service. Sets, food, and waste carry their own emissions. Those can be cut, or properly offset by real reductions elsewhere rather than tree-planting pledges, but the industry is a long way from doing it.

    So how the world generates its energy matters most. Cutting filmmaking's footprint, from discarded AI takes to diesel generators, limits the damage while that work gets done.

    [In practice]

    How AI filmmaking actually works

    Nobody generates a shot once. How many takes a production throws away changes the answer more than which model it uses.

    How people workTakes per kept shotAI shooting ratioExample

    Professionals mostly start from prepared images and animate them, a workflow called image-to-video. In long-form studio work, AI shows up mainly in previz, crowds, environments, and individual VFX shots. House of David generated about 20 takes for every AI shot it used.

    Seedance's own benchmark rates image-to-video clips lower, clip for clip, than text-to-video. Prepared images help with continuity across shots, which benchmarks don't measure, so the calculator leaves the shooting ratio up to you.

    "Unlimited" plans deserve suspicion. Artlist, Runway, Higgsfield, and Krea have all run unlimited Seedance windows, which make one more take free to you while its energy stays exactly the same. This site counts generated seconds, never spend.

    Money is its own question. Generation is cheap, and people are the expensive part. Svedka's AI Super Bowl ad is the cautionary tale: the brand's CMO said it "didn't save much money and certainly not a ton of time."

    Biggest lever

    Draft first

    65–80%

    less energy per kept shot when you explore at 480p and render only the keepers at full resolution. A Seedance 2.5 draft costs 18% of a 1080p render. Cheap drafts can invite more drafts, though.

    Use carefully

    Generate lower, upscale locally

    0.7 Wh

    at the chip to upscale a 5-second clip 2× with Real-ESRGAN, measured on a base M4 Mac mini. A diffusion upscaler such as SeedVR2 can use about 125 Wh for the same clip, which wipes out the saving.

    Counterintuitive

    Fast beats frugal

    21 Wh

    for a 5-second Wan 2.1 clip on an RTX 4090 desktop, against 50.5 Wh at the chip alone on a base M4 Mac mini. The 4090 draws 20 to 30 times the power but finishes in under 3 minutes instead of 3 hours 25, so it uses less than half the energy.

    Local or cloud?

    Running a model on your own computer skips the data center, but that alone saves little. What matters is how big the model is and how fast the hardware finishes the job. The same small Wan model uses about 3.5 Wh per generated second on a data-center GPU, 4 Wh on an RTX 4090 desktop, and 10 Wh at the Mac mini's chip alone. The big savings come from small, distilled models, and those are the ones with visibly lower quality.

    Energy per second of generated video, on a log scale. Each gridline is 10×. Teal runs in a data center. Violet runs on your own machine.

    Local generation is worth it when all of these hold:

    • The model is small. LTX-Video made 5 seconds on the Mac mini for 0.57 Wh at the chip, a few hundred times less than Seedance 2.5 uses for the same length. A mid-size Wan model at 720p on a 4090 uses about 14 Wh a second, as much as MiniMax H3 in the cloud.
    • The hardware is fast. A GPU that finishes in minutes beats a frugal chip that runs for hours, because the whole machine draws power for as long as the job runs.
    • The quality is enough for the job. Open models that fit on a desktop GPU or a Mac trail the cloud leaders. In Artificial Analysis's blind-vote arena, LTX-2.5 scores about 1,055 and LTX-2.3 about 960–975, against 1,210–1,229 for Seedance 2.0, MiniMax H3, and Wan 3.0, so viewers pick the cloud model about 70–80% of the time. That makes local models useful for previz, animatics, and look tests, and only marginally useful for finished shots. If a weaker model needs more takes, or the shot gets remade in the cloud anyway, the saving shrinks or disappears.
    • Your power is cleaner. Seedance's international API runs on Malaysia's grid at about 600 g CO2e per kWh, against about 380 g for the US average and 230 g for Los Angeles's utility.

    The cloud has efficient options too. MiniMax H3 Max, a distilled model, uses roughly 3 Wh a second and scores 1,227. The one open model in that top group, MiniMax H3, needs four data-center GPUs, so it isn't a home option.

    Guided platforms like Krea

    Krea hosts every Seedance and MiniMax tier alongside cheap exploration tools: a realtime image costs about 1/2,000 of a Seedance clip in Krea's own units, and its node editor shows the cost of each step and skips work that hasn't changed. If you use that layer to settle on a shot before paying for premium video, it plausibly saves energy. Nobody has measured it, though, and realtime video and agents keep GPUs busy for the whole session.

    Voices and music

    Local text-to-speech and music models use under 2 Wh per minute of audio. Turning off a video model's own audio saves only what the model spends on it, which is about 21–34% of compute for LTX-2. Seedance 2.5 and MiniMax H3 generate audio in the same pass and don't discount silent clips.

    [Method]

    How the numbers work

    No video company publishes energy per clip, so the AI side is always an estimate. Here's what each estimate rests on.

    AI energy, carbon, and water

    Energy per generated second starts from GPU time. For Seedance 2.5, three methods agree at 17–24 Wh per second on the GPUs at 720p: OpenRouter's latency benchmark, a calibration against Jegham, Gamazaychikov, and Luccioni's 2026 estimate for Seedance 1.5, and ByteDance's reported 70% gross margin. We use 18 Wh. MiniMax H3 is anchored on MiniMax's own benchmark for the open-weight model.

    We then add servers, idle capacity, and data-center cooling (Google's 2025 serving study found the full stack uses about 1.7× the GPU energy), apply the grid where the model runs, and add hardware manufacturing and a 5% share for training. Water counts cooling at the data center plus the water used to generate its electricity.

    Ranges combine each factor's own uncertainty. The AI shooting ratio isn't part of the range because you set it.

    We timed MiniMax H3 through MiniMax's own API, which reports when each job starts running. A 2K clip took about 1.7× as long as 768p, close to the 1.5× we assume, and 15-second clips cost about 15% less per second than 5-second clips. Timing Seedance 2.5 through resellers measured mostly queueing, so it can't pin down Seedance's compute.

    Measured on a Mac mini

    The most useful result is the counterintuitive one. For the one heavy model, Wan 2.1, the Mac mini used 50.5 Wh at the chip for a 5-second clip. An RTX 4090 desktop running the same settings uses about 21 Wh at the wall, less than half, because it finishes more than 70 times faster. Small distilled models were cheap on the Mac: LTX-Video made 5 seconds for 0.57 Wh.

    To check the local figures, we ran five open models in ComfyUI on a base M4 Mac mini with 16 GB of memory and logged the chip's own power (CPU, GPU, and neural engine) once a second with macOS's powermetrics. That leaves out memory, storage, the fan, and the power supply, so energy at the wall socket is higher. Each figure is the mean of up to five runs after a warm-up.

    Conventional production

    Benchmarks come from the Sustainable Entertainment Alliance (US studios, 2023–24), BAFTA albert (UK), AdGreen (advertising), and Ecoprod's Carbon'Clap (France). They measure different things: the US studio benchmark counts fuel, utilities, flights without their high-altitude warming effect, and hotels, while the others also count food, materials, waste, and post. We raise the US studio figures by 1.3× to close that gap.

    Industry benchmarks leave out VFX rendering, and it isn't small. Weta's cloud rendering for Avatar: The Way of Water alone comes to roughly 8–28 GWh. Rendering a single VFX shot takes about 0.15–5 MWh.

    Avatar also shows how much the circumstances change the answer. Weta is in Wellington, and 87% of New Zealand's electricity came from renewables in 2022. But New Zealand had no AWS region then, so the cloud rendering ran in Australian data centers. On Australia's grid it comes to roughly 4,800–16,200 t CO2e. The same work on New Zealand's grid would have been about 850–2,900 t. Amazon says renewable purchases matched 90% of its electricity use in 2022, which shrinks the figure under market-based accounting, but the servers still drew from Australia's grid.

    The production side of the same films was unusually clean. Lightstorm ran the sequels' two Manhattan Beach stages entirely on its own 960 kW rooftop solar array and served 55,000 plant-based meals. That doesn't cancel the rendering, but it shows how far one production's choices can move its footprint, and how little an industry average says about any single film.
